The Form Letter Problem

What 40,000 near-identical public comments taught us about listening at scale.

Riley Sandoval · June 11, 2026 · 14 min read

In March, the Federal Docket Management System logged its forty-thousandth comment on a proposed rule about broadband buildout reporting. Reading the docket end to end takes about eleven days. We know because we did it — or rather, we wrote a small pipeline that did, and then we checked its work by hand.

What we found says less about broadband than about the machinery of public comment itself. The comment period is one of the few places where the public formally touches the administrative state, and it is buckling under exactly the kind of scale that software is supposed to be good at.

What the docket actually contained

Of 40,806 comments, fewer than seven percent were individually composed. The rest arrived in waves, traceable to a handful of advocacy campaigns whose supporters submitted a suggested text with little or no modification.

The table understates the clustering. Even within the individually written pool, semantic similarity puts most comments into a dozen argument families. The docket looks like forty thousand voices; it reads like forty.

Deduplication is a policy choice

The standard technical response is to deduplicate: collapse the copies, count the originals, move on. Our pipeline does this in a few lines.

import hashlib, re

def fingerprint(comment: str) -> str:
    """Cluster near-duplicates: collapse whitespace, strip
    salutations and signatures, hash the remainder."""
    body = re.sub(r"\s+", " ", comment.lower())
    body = re.sub(r"^(dear|to whom).*?[,:]", "", body)
    return hashlib.sha256(body.encode()).hexdigest()[:12]

But where you draw the line between same comment and different comment is not a technical parameter. Strip too little and a campaign looks like a groundswell of independent writing. Strip too much and you erase the one paragraph a retired line worker added about her county's actual coverage map — the paragraph that was, for this docket, the single most useful sentence an agency received.

A form letter is not noise. It is a signature on someone else's sentence — and signatures are data.

What agencies could do instead

Treat the campaign and the annotation as different objects. Count the signatures as what they are — a petition — and route the added paragraphs to reviewers as what they are: testimony. The tooling for this split is not exotic; most of it is the fingerprint function above plus a diff against the template.

We are publishing the pipeline, a labeled sample of the docket, and a short methods note. If you work on comment systems — inside government or outside — we would like to compare results.
